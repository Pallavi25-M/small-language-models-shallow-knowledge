Ivan Alekseyevich Bunin ( BOO-neen; alternatively:  BOO-nin; Russian: Иван Алексеевич Бунин, IPA: [ɪˈvan‿əlʲɪkˈsʲejɪvʲɪdʑ‿ˈbunʲɪn] ; 22 October [O.S. 10 October] 1870 – 8 November 1953) was a Russian writer. He became the first Russian writer awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature, in 1933.  He carried on the classical Russian traditions in the writing of prose and poetry. The texture of his poems and stories, sometimes referred to as "Bunin brocade", is considered  to be one of the richest in the language.
Best known for his short novels The Village (1910) and Dry Valley (1912), autobiographical novel The Life of Arseniev (1933, 1939), short stories collection Dark Avenues (1946) and 1917–1918 diary Cursed Days (1926), Bunin was a revered figure among Russian émigrés, European critics, and many of his fellow writers, who viewed him as an heir to the tradition of realism in Russian literature established by Tolstoy and Chekhov.
